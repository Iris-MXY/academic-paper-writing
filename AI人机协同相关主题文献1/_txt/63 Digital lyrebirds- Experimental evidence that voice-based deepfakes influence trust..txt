   Digital Lyrebirds: Experimental Evidence that
      Voice-based Deep Fakes Influence Trust

We consider the pairing of audio chatbot technologies with voice-based deep fakes, i.e., voice clones, examin-
ing the potential of this combination to induce consumer trust. We report on a set of controlled experiments
based on the investment game, evaluating how voice cloning and chatbot disclosure jointly affect participants’
trust, reflected by their willingness to play with an autonomous, AI-enabled partner. We observe evidence
that voice-based agents garner significantly greater trust from subjects when imbued with a clone of the
subject’s voice. Recognizing that these technologies present not only opportunities, but also the potential for
misuse, we further consider the moderating impact of AI-disclosure, a recent regulatory proposal advocated
by some policymakers. We find no evidence that AI disclosure attenuates the trust-inducing effect of voice
clones. Finally, we explore underlying mechanisms and contextual moderators for the trust-inducing effects,
with an eye toward informing future efforts to manage and regulate voice-cloning applications. We find that
a voice clone’s effects operate at least in part by inducing a perception of homophily and that the effects
are increasing in the clarity and quality of generated audio. Implications of these results for consumers,
policymakers, and society are discussed.

Key words : Artificial intelligence; deep fakes; voice cloning; generative AI; hypernudging; trust; social
  engineering.

1.     Introduction
Generative Artificial Intelligence (Generative AI) technologies have advanced rapidly of
late, yielding unprecedented progress across various domains. Generative models are pre-
dictive models typically rooted in deep neural network architectures, e.g., generative adver-
sarial networks (Goodfellow et al. 2014), variational autoencoders (Kingma et al. 2019),
transformers (Vaswani et al. 2017), which are trained with the goal of producing new,
previously unseen data samples that closely resemble authentic samples that appear in a
training dataset. These models have been deployed for a variety of exciting applications,
from the development of novel statistical methods (Athey et al. 2019, Bennett et al. 2020),
to the automated production of news headlines (Longoni et al. 2022), the synthesis of
online reviews (Li and Tuzhilin 2019), and the crafting of digital art (Ghosh and Fossas
2022, Zhou and Lee 2024). One prominent use case relates to the synthesis of personalized
content, which may be used to personalize and thereby improve a customer experience.
We explore that possibility here, in the context of audio-based consumer interactions,
examining the trust-inducing effects of personalized generative voice models that clone a
consumer’s own voice (a form of audio deepfake).
     Audio deepfakes, which seek to emulate an individual’s voice signature (Chesney and
Citron 2019, Almutairi and Elgibreen 2022, Mcuba et al. 2023), have recently attracted a
great deal of attention in popular culture due in large part to rapid advancements in the
open-source community. New models and tools are frequently deployed that any individual
can use, at little to no cost, to generate synthetic voice samples that are often difficult
to distinguish from a modeled speaker’s true voice. These technologies are set to have a
massive impact on a variety of industries over the coming years. For instance, McKinsey
estimates that the market for automated customer service call interactions will roughly
double, reaching $9 Billion by 2030, and has forecast that a large component of that
market will comprise of personalized, autonomous audio interactions rooted in generative
AI (Agarwal et al. 2023). Further, these technologies have obvious applications for the
synthesis of personalized voice in audio advertisements, e.g., on streaming music services
(Huang et al. 2018), and for voice-over in video advertisements (Allison et al. 2022).
     Although these technologies have a very large potential to personalize and thereby
improve customer voice interactions, they of course also bring the potential for deception

     Bearing the above in mind, we evaluate here the potential application of voice cloning

an individual’s willingness to trust. Bearing in mind the possible negative applications of
these techniques, we also consider the likely efficacy of recent regulatory proposals toward
mandated AI disclosure, as a possible means of mitigating any trust-inducing influence of
voice clones. More formally, we address the following research questions: To what extent
do generative Deep Fakes induce individuals’ to trust a voice-based agent? Fur-
ther, does disclosure of a voice-based agent’s autonomous nature mitigate any
trust-inducing effects of voice-cloning?

2.      Literature Review
2.1.      Aesthetics, Algorithms & Trust
A long stream of research has looked at the interface between humans and algorithms
(Brunswik 1955, Dawes et al. 1989, Karelaia and Hogarth 2008). As algorithms have become
more prevalent in many sectors of industry and our society, human-algorithm interactions
are now a topic of interest in many fields of study, including Marketing (Castelo et al. 2019,
Luo et al. 2019), Psychology (Dietvorst et al. 2018, Logg et al. 2019), Computer Science
(Yeomans et al. 2019), and Economics (Kleinberg et al. 2017). Within the body of work
on this topic, a particularly relevant stream of research focuses on human appreciation
of, and aversion to algorithms. Findings in that literature highlight that the perception of
algorithms varies greatly across contexts. For example, individuals may be more likely to

appreciate algorithms in contexts characterized by decision-support, as compared to nego-
tiation or algorithmic management (Möhlmann et al. 2021). Further, individuals may be
less likely to appreciate algorithms in objective tasks, e.g., numeric forecasts (Logg et al.
2019), providing directions, or predicting the weather (Castelo et al. 2019), as opposed to
tasks perceived as requiring more subjective judgement (Lee 2018), e.g., music recommen-
dations (Yeomans et al. 2019), Human Resources decisions (Castelo et al. 2019, Tambe
et al. 2019), or medical diagnoses (Promberger and Baron 2006). A broad take-away from
this body of work, however, is that humans’ perception of algorithms is highly contextual,
depending upon the individual, as well as the setting and nature of the interaction.
    Both IS literature and HCI literature have examined the role of design aesthetics, as
a determinant of trust in technology, specifically in relation to interactions with websites
(Cyr et al. 2009, Liu and Goodhue 2012, Jiang et al. 2016) and robotics (Hegel et al. 2009,
Tractinsky 2015, Mori 1970). A notable phenomenon that has come out of that line of
research is that dealing with the Uncanny Valley (Mori 1970), which refers to the unease
people experience when confronted with a humanoid robot or computer-generated charac-
ter that appears and behaves almost, but not quite, as a human (McGinn and Torre 2019).
A related result has been identified in more recent HCI research, which has demonstrated
that AI systems imbued with voices similar to a user’s friends and family members tend
to be perceived as more ’eerie’, yet they are also, nonetheless more persuasive (Chan et al.
2021). Might a system imbued with a listener’s own voice exhibit similar persuasive influ-
ence? This is an important question; although voice samples from a listener’s friends and
family members might not be readily accessible to an AI system operator, samples of the
listener’s own voice often are. And, there are good reasons to believe that a listener’s own
voice would also likely be persuasive.
    First, while an individual may readily identify the voice of a close social connection,
prior work has found that individuals typically have difficulty recognizing their own voice
when played from a recording (Rousey and Holzman 1967). Self-recognition is difficult
because one typically does not hear their own voice emitted from an external source;
rather, one typically hears their voice emitted from their own mouth, while the sound
also resonates through their skull (Pörschmann 2000). This has a moderating effect on
voice perception, such that the way we typically hear our own voice differs from how it
is experienced by others or when we hear it played from a recording. Because one’s own

                                                                                            7

voice is difficult to recognize (particularly absent any advance indication that one might
be hearing themselves), voice-clones hold the prospect of eliciting persuasion and trust,
while avoiding the perception of ’eerieness’.
  Second, one’s own voice is likely to induce perceptions of homophily and similarity
attraction. Similarity attraction is a well-documented phenomenon in social psychology
(Byrne 1961). This occurs when individuals appraise others as being similar to themselves
in some personal dimension, such as facial attributes (Bailenson et al. 2008), demographics
(Banikiotes and Neimeyer 1981, Hu et al. 2008), or attitudes (Singh et al. 2017, Yeong
Tan and Singh 1995). Although initially observed in face-to-face interpersonal contexts,
similarity-based attraction has been shown to manifest in several technological contexts
as well, including social media (Adamopoulos et al. 2018, Aral et al. 2009), computer-
mediated communication more generally (Kaptein et al. 2014) and AI agents (Nass and
Lee 2001, Nass and Moon 2000, Nass et al. 1997).
  Although there are many explanations for similarity attraction, a dominant explanation
for the phenomenon is the reinforcement-affect model (Byrne and Clore 1970). This model
postulates that people enjoy experiencing positive stimuli (Byrne and Clore 1970) and
actively seek them. Since interactions with similar individuals tend to go more smoothly,
as opposed to dissimilar stimuli, individuals are drawn toward stimuli congruent with these
attributions (Berscheid and Hatfield 1978). For example, when individuals hear a similar
voice, they associate the sound with subsequent positive conversations, as there is already
a basis for common ground. Prior research in psychology has also reported consistent
evidence that individuals form impressions about trustworthiness in a rapid and automatic
(visceral) manner, responding to highly superficial, aesthetic features of another party’s
face (Holmes and Robins 2007, Todorov et al. 2009) or voice (McAleer et al. 2014, Belin
et al. 2017) to subconsciously arrive at “approach or avoid” assessments in a matter of
milliseconds. Given the prior evidence in psychology, it is perhaps unsurprising that work
in HCI has observed that AI-enabled agents are more persuasive or trust-inducing when
imbued with the voices of friends or family-members (Chan et al. 2021).
  Early work experimenting with computerized voice and text-based chatbots documented
that experiment participants would often attribute human-like qualities and social motives,
despite stating explicitly that they were aware the other party was entirely artificial (Nass
and Lee 2001, Nass and Moon 2000, Nass et al. 1997). To trigger these attributions,



                  Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4839606
8

researchers would manipulate aesthetics like para-linguistic vocal cues that better align
with the individual’s own (Nass and Lee 2001), and voice accents that mirror those of
the human counterpart (Dahlbäck et al. 2007). Consistent findings have been reported in
more recent work evaluating trust. For example in an experiment investigating human-AI
collaboration, Trainer et al. (2020) found that subjects preferred to work with AI-enabled
agents whose avatars exhibited similar racial and gender characteristics to themselves.
Further, those authors observed that this effect arose because subjects trusted those agents
more, even though the agents were very clearly robots, and not human. These prior findings
suggest that an AI’s similarity to their counterpart can greatly influence end-user behavior
and that mere awareness of the AI’s nature may fail to mitigate those effects.
    If any persuasive effects do derive from a voice-clone, a natural question then, from
a consumer protection standpoint, is how one might mitigate that influence. The above
points raise questions about the likely efficacy of relying on AI disclosure as a means of
managing or regulating the potential effects of a voice clone.

2.2.   AI Disclosure & Decision Risk
Management literature addressing the use of autonomous agents in customer-facing job
roles is relatively rare, but speaks to a possible intervention for mitigating the likely per-
suasive, trust-inducing influence of voice-clones. An important design consideration when
deploying these IT artifacts in a managerial setting is whether to disclose their autonomous
nature. However, findings related to disclosure interventions are somewhat mixed.
    For example, recent work by Luo et al. (2019) explored a financial institution’s use of
voice-based agents to solicit customer loan renewals. Employing a field experiment, the
authors randomized customers to receive calls from voice-based chatbots or human employ-
ees, and further randomized whether the autonomous nature of an agent was disclosed to
the consumer at the outset of the call. The authors found that, although loan renewals were
equally likely using a voice-based chatbot or a human employee, when the autonomous
nature of the chatbot was disclosed upfront, subjects were nearly 80% less likely to renew
their loan. These findings would seem to suggest that consumers’ trust in AI algorithms
may be heavily dependent upon awareness that they are in fact dealing with an algorithm.
In another study, Tong et al. (2021) utilized AI as a manager, employing it to provide
managerial feedback to employees. Those authors found a similar negative response to AI



                   Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4839606
                                                                                            9

feedback, but the effect was notably attenuated among more established, seasoned employ-
ees. In contrast, Schanke et al. (2021) explored autonomous customer service agents in a
textual medium, where the AI agents acted on behalf of a retailer to negotiate and accept
used clothing buy-back offers from consumers. Unlike Luo et al. (2019) and Tong et al.
(2021), Schanke et al. (2021) found that consumers perceived chatbots as more likeable
when their autonomous nature was explicitly disclosed. Further, Mozafari et al. (2022)
explored user perceptions of agents in a conversation, and discovered that errors were
viewed less negatively when the user was aware the other party was an autonomous chat-
bot. Finally, in a brand-related context, Kirkby et al. (2023) observed no negative impacts
of AI disclosure on downstream brand attributions. The highly variable consequences of
AI disclosure suggest that the relationship may vary a great deal with context. This is, in
part, a reason that we seek to explore the trust-inducing effects of conversational AI, and
how it varies with AI disclosure in a highly controlled, de-contextualized setting.
  The prior conflicting results in the literature about the impact of disclosure raise ques-
tions about the potential efficacy of disclosure as a policy intervention here. Further, as
mentioned earlier, in our setting we have reason to expect that disclosure might not miti-
gate the trust-inducing effects of similarity attraction in autonomous agents, because the
trust-inducing effects we anticipate are likely to occur mindlessly(Nass and Moon 2000),
as a visceral response (Todorov et al. 2009, Belin et al. 2017), evoking trust regardless of
individuals’ conscious consideration of the other party’s traits.
  A second factor that prior literature highlights, which likely plays into AI-trust, is the
degree of risk involved in a decision. Numerous studies have found that people are more
likely to reject decision-support from algorithms in risky or uncertain environments, such as
financial investing, medical decision-making, or demand forecasting (Mahmud et al. 2022).
Accordingly, we might anticipate that any persuasive influence of voice-clones would be
attenuated under risk. However, it is important to bear in mind that there are important
differences between scenarios in which algorithms provide decision-support (e.g., medical
diagnosis, or a forecast) versus those in which an autonomous agent is acting on behalf
of a firm, interacting with a customer. In the former scenario, the algorithm and the user
generally share common objectives, something that might not be true in the latter setting.
  A likely more appropriate framework for thinking about consumer trust in an
autonomous agent, acting on behalf of a firm, is that from the HCI literature dealing with



                  Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4839606
10

trust in robotics. A widely accepted definition from that literature states that trust is “the
attitude that an agent will help achieve an individual’s goal in a situation characterized by
uncertainty and vulnerability”(Lee and See 2004). As noted above, design aesthetics have
received extensive study as a driver of human trust in robotic partners.
     The literature notes that an agent that is perceived to be empathetic also tends to
be perceived as more trustworthy, likeable, and caring (Lee 2006). As a result, scholars
in that discipline have suggested anthropomorphic robots present certain dangers from
a design standpoint, because humans may develop an inappropriate level of trust (Lewis
et al. 2018), even in risky situations (Howard and Borenstein 2019). Indeed, some work has
found that human trust in robotic partners is increased when robots bear anthropomorphic
characteristics, e.g., a name, or a human form, even when they have shown themselves to
be error-prone (Ahmad et al. 2019). Extending this line of thought, the notion of similarity
attraction and homophily implies that empathy and thus trust in autonomous agents may
be expected to increase in the presence of aesthetics that imply greater similarity, likeness,
or familiarity to a human evaluator, e.g., the customer. Rather than eliminate the trust-
inducing effects of a voice-clone, a very real potential exists that such effects may instead
operate at their strongest under scenarios of elevated risk.
     To better tease out these factors, we pursue an alternative research design, in the form of
a controlled economic experiment. This methodology has been used to better understand
human decision-making biases and strategic behavior (e.g., trust, cooperation, negotiation)
in a variety of scenarios (Camerer 2018, March 2019, Thaler 2018, Smith et al. 1988). While
behavioral economics, as a discipline, has not focused on human-machine interaction, the
methods that economists have developed in that field have frequently been leveraged by
scholars of HCI (March 2019, McCabe et al. 2001, Sanfey et al. 2003, Kiesler et al. 1996,
Torta et al. 2013, Lee et al. 2011) and Organizational Behavior (Adam et al. 2018), to
improve our understanding of these interactions and design considerations.

3.     Methods
3.1.     Experiment Design
We explore the influence of voice-clones on human trust in an AI-enabled partner, employ-
ing an experimental design first introduced by Charness and Dufwenberg (2006). The
experiment, a variant of the investment (trust) game, was developed to test how commu-
nication influences trust between two subjects. In the original game design, articulated in



                     Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4839606

Figure 1, subjects are first ”matched” to a human playing partner and randomly assigned
to play the role of agent A or B. In the original treatment condition, subjects playing
the role of B would have the opportunity to pass a written message to party A, stating
whatever they like, e.g., superfluous comments, promises, etc. Subsequently, party A would
decide whether they wished to play the game with B (choosing ”in” or ”out”). If party A
decided to play, B could choose to walk away with some money, leaving A with nothing,
or B could choose to roll a die. Choosing to roll, the final payoff to both parties would
depend on the resulting number that was rolled, with a 5 in 6 chance of a positive payoff
for both players. This study design enabled Charness and Dufwenberg (2006) to examine
how communication would influence the decisions of both A (whether to trust B), and B
(how to behave upon receiving that trust).

4.     Empirical Approach & Data
4.1.    Pilot Study
Before launching our main experiment, we began by conducting a pilot study, to ensure
the infrastructure was working properly, to confirm the logistics of execution, to under-
take manipulation and mechanism checks, and to assess any demand effects. More specif-
ically, we ensured the proper operation of the real-time mechanism for voice cloning by
comparing generated audio samples with subjects’ true voices, using industry-standard
voice-comparison tools. As described below, we also performed a manipulation check for
the disclosure treatment, assessing whether subjects perceived it as intended. Finally, we
confirmed the manipulation of our voice clone treatment, assessing whether subjects would
perceive the resulting voices’ similarity, and whether, in turn, that would induce subjects
to perceive that their assigned partner was more similar to themselves, demographically.
     Disclosure Manipulation: To evaluate the effect of our disclosure manipulation, we
recruited N = 465 subjects, and randomized them into disclosure (N=234) and non-
disclosure (N=231) conditions. After subjects completed the game, we asked them to
provide free-form textual responses to the following questions: ”Please describe the other
party playing this game with you.” Based on subjects’ responses, we examined the rate at
which subjects described their partner as autonomous, computerized, or AI-enabled. The
raw textual descriptions our subjects provided about their game partners can be found
in Appendix C. We report cross tabulations of the results below in Table 1. As expected,
subjects were more likely to describe their partner as bot-like in the disclosure condition.
Of the 234 subjects in the disclosure condition, 48.7% described the other party as com-
puterized, AI, not human, or algorithmic, as compared to 37, or 16%, in the non-disclosure
condition. This difference demonstrates that, while some individuals in the non-disclosure
condition do clearly suspect that the other party is an autonomous agent, individuals
are nonetheless much more likely to describe the other party as such under disclosure.
While this difference may reflect a simply greater willingness or comfort with disclosing
pre-existing suspicions, the result is at least consistent with the idea that disclosure does
manipulate individuals’ beliefs about the other party’s autonomous nature.

     Robustness: Randomization Inference
     Randomization inference (RI)-based tests are less susceptible to influential, high-leverage
observations (Young 2019). We conducted 10,000 permutations. With each iteration, we re-

the test statistic associated with each coefficient at each step, rather than the coefficient.
After completing all 10,000 iterations, we calculated empirical p-values associated with
each coefficient as the fraction of all test statistics observed under the null (i.e., across
our permutations of the data) that were less extreme than the statistic obtained in the
regressions using the true data. In Table 6 we once again report our point estimates for all
coefficients under each specification again, this time providing each parameter’s empirical
p-value in square brackets, obtained from the RI-procedure.

Our work provides a first consideration of the potential value that real-time voice cloning
technology may have for eliciting consumer trust in autonomous, voice-based agents, par-
ticularly those operating in customer-facing roles on behalf of a firm. Our experiments indi-
cate that the use of voice clones significantly increases individuals’ trust in an autonomous

only likely to grow with time, as the technologies improve.
     At the same time, exploring possible approaches to managing possible misuses of these
technologies, we considered the possible mitigating influence of AI disclosure. Unfortu-
nately, we found no evidence that the trust-inducing effects of a clone were mitigated by
explicit disclosure of the agent’s autonomous nature. Further, additional analyses involving
independently randomized risk treatments were suggestive that the trust-inducing effects
of voice clones may be strongest in decision-making contexts characterized by high-risk
(see Appendix E), suggesting that future work can and should delve more deeply into other
possible strategies for mitigation and management of these tools.
     That voice clones induce greater trust is a finding that complements prior results in
the literature on AI disclosure. Our finding that disclosure does not necessarily undermine
trust is consistent with results reported by Schanke et al. (2021) and Mozafari et al.
(2022), though in contrast (at least superficially) to those reported by Luo et al. (2019)
and Tong et al. (2021). Context is likely to play a major role here. For example, whereas
Luo et al. (2019) examined AI disclosure in the context of unsolicited sales calls, our study
and that by Schanke et al. (2021) examine a setting wherein individuals have opted into
the interaction. A more rigorous examination of the literature and possible sources of this
heterogeneity are worth exploring in future work.
     Our work also builds on prior work dealing with the efficacy and influence of face morphs
in visual advertisements, i.e., self-morphs, wherein elements of a consumer’s own face are



                       Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4839606
                                                                                                                      25

integrated into facial images within advertisements. Despite past lab-based evidence that
self-morphs induce greater trust, even when users do not recognize the presence of their
own face, recent work attempting to adapt self-morphs into a field setting has observed no
evidence of improved advertisement performance (Samat et al. 2018). Our work suggests
that this strategy may be more effective in audio settings using voice clones. Self-morphs are
relatively subtle by design, drawing on only some elements of a consumer’s face, presumably
in an effort to avoid the consumer recognizing attempts at manipulation. In contrast,
voice clones attempt to mimic the consumer’s voice completely. The reason this approach
is feasible in the context of voice is that even a perfect clone is unlikely to induce a
sense of ‘eerieness’ on the part of consumers, because individuals typically face difficulty
recognizing their own voice (Rousey and Holzman 1967).19 Other, contextual factors may
also contribute to our finding that this strategy works with voice clones. For example, it
may be that trust-inducing effects are amplified in our setting because it is a dynamic
social interaction. Alternatively, the stronger result in our setting may be driven by the fact
that the communication and interaction between parties in our setting are purely voice-
based, with voice serving as the primary and only source of social information (Walther
and Tidwell 1995), making it very salient to subjects.
     Our work takes a first step toward understanding the implications of audio deepfakes
deployed within business applications. As these technologies are becoming a much more
important element of day to day life, it is increasingly important that we understand
the potential ways they may be used to influence customer behavior. Audio deepfake
technologies can be deployed to implement a form of hypernudging in voice-based contexts
(Yeung 2019, Morozovaite 2023). The strategy we examine could be put to beneficial use
by organizations, to personalize consumers’ voice-based experiences and further enhance
consumers’ social interactions with voice-based AI agents.
     Indeed, the breadth of contexts in which voice clones might be deployed is growing as
more companies begin to collect consumer voice data, and companies begin to implement
conversational, audio-based consumer touch points (Grewal et al. 2021). As noted in the
introduction, conversational AI stands to have a huge impact on applications of interactive

7.       Limitations & Conclusion
While this study provides novel insights, our work is subject to certain limitations. First,
our experiment occurs in a controlled online setting. As such, the degree to which our
results generalize to relevant field settings (e.g., sales conversion, lead generation, etc.)
remains an open question. Although our controlled setting provides us the ability to tease
apart mechanisms and potential sources of influence, future work should be done in field
contexts to quantify these trust-inducing effects using measures of more direct relevance
to practice.
     That said, our experiment design is notably relevant to and at least superficially repre-
sentative of many real-world consumer settings. In most settings that involve AI-enabled
customer service agents, the agent acts as a delegate (Libai et al. 2020), working on an
organization’s behalf to assist the consumer. For example, a customer might interact with
an automated voice-based agent to perform various customer support tasks, e.g., change
a flight booking, update dinner plans, or even renew a bank loan (Luo et al. 2019). In
all such scenarios, consumers are uncertain whether the agent will perform the task prop-
erly. Our game design parallels such real world contexts. Charness and Dufwenberg (2006,
2010) explicitly designed their game to study exactly these scenarios, involving hidden-
information, where an anonymous party’s actions have implications for the subject’s utility
a priori.
     Second, the experiment focuses on trust in a meaningful economic scenario. While there
are several conceptualizations of trust that have been employed in prior literature for the
design of technology artifacts (e.g., Benbasat and Wang 2005), the version we employ here
20
     https://finance.yahoo.com/news/global-call-centers-business-analysis-090800828.html




                         Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4839606
                                                                                            27

has a long history in economics for understanding individual decision-making in real-time
choice settings. In short, the design of the game is such that it leverages pay-offs and choice
structures that elicit decision-making that closely mirrors scenarios that would arise in real-
world economic choices (Becker et al. 1964). This trust variable assesses whether the subject
deems the other party as credible in a one shot economic scenario. Future work could
evaluate alternative conceptualizations of trust, like trust in the artifact’s functionality
(Choung et al. 2023).
  Another limitation of our work is its focus on North American English-speakers. It is not
clear whether our results may generalize across cultures. As noted earlier, Luo et al. (2019)
found that AI disclosure negatively impacts consumer interest in a loan renewal, within
a Chinese banking context. Further work on AI disclosure might thus seek to evaluate
cultural (and other demographic) differences in individuals’ receptivity to AI agents.
  Finally, our experimental setting limits our ability to assess the true beliefs of subjects,
particularly under the non-disclosure condition. While we do observe that subjects in the
Disclosure condition were more likely to describe their partner using terminology more
indicative of a bot or a computer, it remains unclear whether subjects truly believed their
assigned partner to be human. Asking subjects to report their belief of whether the other
party was a bot via survey, as we do in Appendix D, presents inherent challenges for
measuring true perceptions, because merely by reading the question, respondents may be
induced to reflect on their experience and reassess, potentially tainting their response.
Further study is thus needed to understand the degree to which human subjects are capable
of discerning that their partner is an AI-enabled autonomous, synthetic agent, and how
that verified perception moderates the effects of a voice clone.
  This work will ideally aid both practitioners and policymakers in developing a clearer
understanding of the business applications of audio-based deepfakes, as well as the potential
value of certain policy and regulatory approaches to addressing risks of misuse, e.g., AI
disclosure. It is our hope that this work represents only a first step toward a broader
stream of literature in management and policy, one that will ideally explore a broader
set of potential impacts, as well as appropriate policy and managerial responses, in light
of the ongoing rapid advancements and applications of generative AI technologies in the
business sphere. Further, it is our hope that this research can spurn further scholarship in
the domain of voice-based consumer interactions.

Appendix B           Controlling for Message Content
Below we evaluate whether specific message content may drive our results. A possible
concern might be that audio quality varies systematically with message content (perhaps
because the generative neural network breaks down with certain phonemes). To address
this possibility, we estimate our LPM that relates Opt-In to the Voice Clone treatment,
while conditioning on a message fixed effect, which controls for the specific message a user
received (randomly drawn from those documented in the experiment by Charness and
Dufwenberg (2006)). We find that the Voice Clone effect persists, implying that our results
are not driven by specific messages being communicated; rather, the results are driven
purely by the voice aesthetic.

Appendix D         Perceived Humanness of Other Party
A limitation of our research design is that it is difficult to decipher a subject’s true belief
about the autonomous versus human nature of their game partner. By asking a participant
at the end of the experiment whether they believe the other party was a human or a bot,
we will very likely influence that belief prior to receiving their response. Participants who
might not have realized, initially, are likely to reflect on their experience and revise their
beliefs upon being prompted with the question. Further, conditioning on any such response
in an empirical test may pose an econometric challenge, because the response may also be
a function of the treatment, making it a bad control.
  With these limitations in mind, we nonetheless conducted an additional experiment in a
slightly different setup, one that did not include an incentivized game design. We randomly
assigned participants to one of three voice conditions: synthetic random voice, synthetic
clone voice, or a real human voice. To implement the real human condition, we paid Prolific
workers to record themselves reading our messages and we then used those recordings in
the new experiment, for participants assigned to the human condition.
  Participants were asked to listen to a randomly selected message (again from among the
set of all messages in the original Charness and Dufwenberg study), where the message
was read either by a voice clone (once again based upon the subject’s phone-based reading
of our consent statement), a randomly chosen synthetic voice, or a real human voice. We
then told subjects that we were using a mix of human and bot voices in our study, and
asked them to state which they believed they had heard in the recorded message. We also
asked subjects to report the level they agreed with the following statements: “the other
party is trustworthy,” on a scale from 1 to 5, with 5 being strongly agree, “the other party
is reliable,” and “the other party is honest.” Here, trustworthiness is our key outcome,
while honesty and reliability might be viewed as precursors to, or dimensions of trust.
  Additionally, we explored possible mechanisms by which a Clone might induce more
trust than a Random synthetic voice, asking subjects to report the extent to which they
agreed with statements conveying various other desirable qualities of the other party. These
other statements included the degree to which the participant felt the other party was:
likable, attractive, familiar, natural, intelligent, warm, nice, or good. Here, it is important
to note that we do not necessarily anticipate a significant increase in the perception of
any of these positive characteristics because, as noted previously, the trust-inducing nature



                   Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=4839606
40

of faces and voices has been shown to be quite visercal and instinctive in nature (rather
than conscious and deliberative) (Todorov et al. 2009, Belin et al. 2017). Additionally,
and consistent with the idea that facial and vocal cues influence trusting perceptions on a
visceral, sub-conscious level, other prior work dealing with perceptions of self-similar faces
was unable to detect evidence that the effects were associated with increased perceptions
of attractiveness or familiarity (DeBruine 2002).
     This experiment was somewhat smaller in scale. We recruited approximately 200 sub-
jects, and we then dropped several who 1) did not play the audio file, 2) had a Qualtrics
recaptcha score below 0.85, 3) had duplicate IP addresses across survey responses, or 4)
exhibited clear evidence of inattention (e.g., providing identical response across all survey
items, e.g., all 1’s). Excluding these subjects, we were left with 172 valid responses.
     Below, we report the results of the experiment. First, we compare the perceptions of a
cloned voice and human benchmark to a random voice on the positive attributes (likeable,
attractive, familiar, etc). In Tables 11 - 13, we report no statistically significant differences
for the cloned versus random voice across these outcomes. Next, in Table 14, we report
our results for the cloned voice on both reliability and honesty outcomes, here we note a
statistically significant positive effect of cloned voice on reliability. Finally, we consider 1)

Appendix E         Moderating Effect of Risk
In addition to voice and disclosure treatments, we also delivered an independently ran-
domized treatment related to risk information.
  We developed our risk treatments based on reference to definitions of risk in the liter-
ature, which defines risk separately from uncertainty (Park and Shapira 2017) and also
articulates its relationship with trust (Das and Teng 2004). Our interventions are defined
as follows: i) subjects in the high-risk condition were informed that Party B had opted to
cooperate and roll just 1 time in their last 6 games; ii) subjects in the no risk information
condition received no information on Party B’s play history; and iii) subjects in the low-
risk condition received information that Party B had opted to cooperate and roll 5 times
out of their last 6 games.
  We evaluate whether and to what degree the trust-inducing effects of a voice clone vary
with the level of risk a subject faces. Specifically, we subset our data, splitting the sample
on the basis of assigned risk information. We then estimate three LPMs, relating Opt-In
to the Voice Clone treatment in each case. The results are presented in Table 16.
  First, considering the intercept term in each column, we find, as we might expect, that
subjects’ probability of Opt-In is lowest in the high-risk condition, in the presence of infor-
mation indicating that Party B rarely cooperates, yet highest in the low-risk condition, the
presence of information that Party B often rolls. Considering the estimated effect of the
Voice Clone in each group, we see that the effect is largest, and only statistically significant,
under the high-risk condition. Specifically, the estimated coefficient nearly doubles com-
pared to those obtained in the No Info and Low Risk conditions. This finding is suggestive
that the influence of voice clones is most prominent under high risk.
  That said, these results need to be interpreted with a great deal of caution, and further
study is needed. We say this because a statistical test that compares the estimates across
these three conditions does not indicate that statistically significant differences in the Voice
Clone’s effects.
