Powered by the increasing predictive capabilities of machine learning algorithms, artificial intelli-
gence (AI) systems have acquired the ability to improve outcomes by overruling human mistakes.
However, to assess the full impact of adding AI oversight, we must understand whether its presence
alters human decision-making. While a large amount of research has focused on how humans
respond when being assisted by AI, very little is known about how humans respond when their
decisions might be overruled by AI. Because being overruled can carry psychological costs (e.g.,
shame and embarrassment of being overruled) and psychological benefits (e.g., relief at having
their mistakes fixed), individuals might alter their decision-making under AI oversight. We pro-
vide the first field evidence that AI oversight carries psychological costs that can impact human
decision-making.
   We investigate one of the highest visibility settings in which AI oversight has occurred: Hawk-
Eye review of umpires in top tennis tournaments. The introduction of Hawk-Eye, a video-based
AI system, paved the way for professional tennis players to challenge umpire calls of whether a
ball had bounced in or out of bounds. When challenged, the umpire’s decision was overturned (or,
in some cases, the point was replayed) if it differed from the prediction of the highly accurate AI.
Additionally, the AI’s prediction of the final landing location of the ball was displayed on a large
screen in the stadium for the umpire, players, and crowd to observe.
   We compare umpire decisions in tournaments before and after the introduction of Hawk-Eye. Our
context offers several empirical advantages, including a testing period during which the technology
was used solely for broadcast and data recording purposes, without a challenge system in place. By
combining two unique data sources and auditing match videos, we construct a novel dataset to
identify umpires’ mistakes across 35 tournaments. Out of the 474,102 bounces registered, we focus
on the 16,812 (3.5%) within 100 mm of the line, where most umpire mistakes occur.

   We find that umpires lowered their overall mistake rate by 8% (1.1 p.p.), in line with rational
inattention given psychological costs to being overruled by AI. This improvement is concentrated in
distant calls (20-100 mm) and situations where the umpire is more prone to distraction (non-serves).
In the most demanding cases, where umpires were likely to be close to their attention limit (serves
within 20 mm of the line), the mistake rate actually increased by 22.9% (7.3 p.p.). This puzzling
result is explained by a behavioral distortion that occurs with AI oversight.
   This behavioral distortion is a sensible outcome of the asymmetric psychological costs of AI
oversight in this setting. For umpires, improperly stopping a point (calling a ball out when it was
actually in) became a new cause of concern with the introduction of Hawk-Eye. Even if a player
successfully challenges such a call, there is no way to return the point to its previous state, so the
rules dictate that the umpire must decide whether to replay the point or award it to the challenger,
placing the chair umpire in a tough and undesired position. When we restrict our analysis to
the closest calls (<20 mm), Type II errors (calling a ball out when it was actually in) decreased
by 4.1 percentage points, while Type I errors (calling a ball in when it was out) increased by 8.6
percentage points, and both changes are statistically significant. We find that for the closest calls,
umpires increased the rate at which they called balls in by 12.6% (6.2 percentage points) after the
introduction of Hawk-Eye, leading to the shift from making Type II errors to Type I errors observed
in Figure 1.
   We structurally estimate a lower bound on the psychological costs being overruled by AI using a
model of rational inattentive umpires. We employ a two-stage approach in which we use decisions
before Hawk-Eye was introduced to recover the perceptual costs of making correct calls, and then
decisions after Hawk-Eye was introduced to determine the psychological costs of being overruled
by AI. The resulting estimates suggest that these psychological costs lead umpires to care much
more about Type II errors (calling a ball out when in) after the introduction of Hawk-Eye review.
   Our results highlight the fact that while introducing an AI system to overrule apparent human
mistakes seems promising in principle, especially since it might motivate higher effort from workers,
there are two primary reasons to approach this with caution. First, there is a free-riding motive in
which the technology can dissuade decision-makers from contributing effort under the belief that

important mistakes will be recognized and corrected by the technology. In our empirical application,
we do not find evidence of this force, but it would likely carry more weight in contexts where
decision-makers prioritize the ultimate outcome over their own performance. The second reason,
which appears to play a significant role in the context we study, is an incentive-misalignment
motive. The implementation guidelines of the oversight mechanism can shift the incentives for the
decision-maker in a way that deteriorates the overall quality of their decisions from the perspective
of the social planner. While distorting the relative costs of errors in tennis may not be deemed
highly consequential from a welfare perspective, there are other contexts, such as medical diagnoses
and judicial courts, where this distortion can carry great importance.
