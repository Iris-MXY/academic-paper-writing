       Conventional optimization algorithms that prescribe order packing instructions (which items to pack
     in which sequence in which box) focus on box volume utilization yet tend to overlook human behavioral
     deviations. We observe that packing workers at the warehouses of Alibaba Group deviate from algorithmic
     prescriptions for 5.8% of packages, and these deviations increase packing time and reduce operational effi-
     ciency. We posit two mechanisms and demonstrate that they result in two types of deviations: (1) information
     deviations stem from workers having more information and in turn better solutions than the algorithm;
     and (2) complexity deviations result from workers’ aversion, inability or discretion to precisely implement
     algorithmic prescriptions.
       We propose a new “human-centric bin packing algorithm” that anticipates and incorporates human devi-
     ations to reduce deviations and improve performance. It predicts when workers are more likely to switch to
     larger boxes using machine learning techniques and then pro-actively adjusts the algorithmic prescriptions of
     those “targeted packages.” We conducted a large-scale randomized field experiment with the Alibaba Group.
     Orders were randomly assigned to either the new algorithm (treatment group) or Alibaba’s original algo-
     rithm (control group). Our field experiment results show that our new algorithm lowers the rate of switching
     to larger boxes from 29.5% to 23.8% for targeted packages and reduces the average packing time of targeted
     packages by 4.5%. This idea of incorporating human deviations to improve optimization algorithms could
     also be generalized to other processes in logistics and operations.

     Key words : Warehouse Operations, Behavioral Operations, Field Experiment, Online Retailing




1.     Introduction
Today, the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) and robotics is revolutionizing business opera-
tions by augmenting human work. Indeed, rather than striving for autonomous automation, we are
entering an age of digital operations where humans can work with AI embodied in algorithms to
execute tasks. Algorithms can improve human work by supporting and augmenting their judgment
and actions. For example, ride-sharing platforms like Uber are driven by algorithms that match
drivers and customers and propose real-time optimized routes. Similarly, Amazon deploys algo-

rithms to drive automated guided vehicles (AGV) within a warehouse and to prescribe a picking
list for warehouse workers to pick and pack from these AGVs.
    Yet users (e.g., drivers and packers) may deviate from the algorithmic prescriptions. We broadly
classify such deviations into two categories in this paper. The first category is information devia-
tion. Information deviations occur when users deviate from infeasible or sub-optimal algorithmic
prescriptions and execute feasible or improved actions because users have more objective informa-
tion than the algorithm. Such deviations exist when the algorithm has insufficient information to
recommend a feasible or optimal prescription. By definition, information deviations are typically
beneficial to operational performance. For example, inaccurate inventory records in an inventory
management system may lead to sub-optimal ordering decisions by the algorithm, but store man-
agers may improve the decisions through information deviations by observing the actual inventory
level (van Donselaar et al., 2010). The second category is complexity deviation, which results from
users’ aversion, inability or discretion to implement the algorithmic prescription rather than the
lack of objective information in the algorithm. Complexity deviations typically happen when algo-
rithmic prescriptions are so complex that users may fail to understand, trust, or execute the pre-
scriptions. As a result, users revert to implementing simpler but sub-optimal decisions instead, even
if the algorithmic prescription is feasible and optimal in practice. Therefore, complexity deviations
typically hurt operational performance.1 For example, human dispatchers may deviate from the
routing prescribed by a vehicle routing algorithm when planning the delivery routes for multiple
drivers, since both dispatchers and drivers usually prefer tours that connect points on the convex
hull in the order of adjacency and avoid complex, overlapping but shorter routes (Rossit et al.,
2019). Overall, the key distinctions between information deviation and complexity deviation are
(1) whether the deviation is due to lack of information in the algorithm design and (2) whether
the deviation improves the algorithmic prescription in feasibility or optimality given the defined
objective function.2
    While these deviations are common in practice, there is little rigorous empirical evidence on how
to encourage or discourage these deviations in the stage of designing algorithms to improve oper-
ational performance. In this paper, we provide the first suggestive field evidence that both types
of deviations may exist in the bin packing process at Alibaba, which is the largest e-commerce
platform in China. We also demonstrate, through a large field experiment, that operational perfor-
mance can improve significantly from re-designing a “human-centric” algorithm that predicts and
anticipates both types of human deviations.

  We first analyze over 1 million packages from July 12, 2018 to July 31, 2018 at Alibaba. We
observe that workers do not always follow the algorithmic instructions when performing their tasks,
which we will refer to as “non-conforming” behavior. Specifically, we find that workers use box
sizes different from the prescribed sizes for 5.8% of the packages. Moreover, the processing time of
these non-conforming packages are 48.3% longer than conforming packages. We further decompose
such non-conforming behaviors into two types.
  First, we show that workers switch to smaller boxes than prescribed in 1.6% of all packages (i.e.,
in more than a quarter of the 5.8% non-conforming packages). We interpret such deviations as
information deviations, since workers can fold or compress some items and pack them in smaller
boxes, while the algorithm lacks the information to identify items that can be folded or compressed.
  Second, in the remaining 5.8% − 1.6% = 4.2% of the packages (i.e., in about three quarters of
the non-conforming packages), workers switch to larger boxes than prescribed. These deviations
may be driven by both information deviation and complexity deviation. Information deviation can
exist in these cases because workers know certain items need wrappers that increase the item sizes,
while the algorithm does not. Complexity deviation may arise when workers fail to organize the
items in the orientations and positions inside the box as the algorithm prescribes, especially for
complex packing tasks. While it is hard to completely differentiate the two types of deviations in
our data, we provide supportive evidence that both deviations may exist and that neither one can
fully explain all switching-to-larger-boxes deviations.
  First, we show that the packages that contain an item that consistently causes workers to switch
to larger boxes only account for less than 0.5% of the packages where workers switch to larger boxes.
This indicates that information deviation exists in the switching-to-large-boxes packages, but it
cannot fully explain all the switching-to-large-boxes deviations. Second, we show that workers are
more likely to switch to larger boxes when tasks are more complex (i.e., when there are multiple
items in the package or when the prescribed fill rate is high). Specifically, the probability of workers
switching to larger boxes is 2.4% for packages containing only one item, but 4.4% for packages with
more than one item. Similarly, the probability of switching to larger boxes is 3.9% for the packages
with prescribed fill rate below 75%, but 9.4% for the packages with prescribed fill rate above 75%.
This suggests that complexity deviation exists for these switching-to-large-boxes packages. Third,
we find that in 35.6% of the packages for which workers switch to larger boxes, workers use boxes
that are at least two sizes larger than prescribed. This provides additional evidence for the existence
of complexity deviation since information deviations (e.g., certain items needing wrappers) are
almost unlikely to cause the required box sizes to be increased by at least 2.
  After analyzing the historical data, we propose a new algorithm that predicts cases, henceforth
referred to as “targeted packages,” where human workers are likely to switch to larger boxes. The

actively adjusts the algorithmic prescriptions for these targeted packages by using a lower maximum
prescribed fill rate. This is likely to cause the algorithm to prescribe a box one size larger than the

two different ways, depending on whether it reduces complexity deviation or information deviation.
If there is complexity deviation, this new algorithm can reduce the difficulty of following the
original bin packing algorithm to pack items by lowering the fill rate (i.e., the complexity) of
targeted packages, which in turn is likely to reduce workers’ probability of switching to larger
boxes and improve workers’ productivity. If there is information deviation, this algorithm may also
systematically identify fragile items that need wrappers from historical human actions and suggest
box sizes that account for the wrapping materials.
    We implemented our new human-centric bin packing algorithm in four of Alibaba’s warehouses
and conducted a large-scale field experiment comparing our human-centric algorithm to the original
algorithm for two weeks from August 27, 2018 to September 9, 2018. Around 14 thousand packages
on average are packed per warehouse per day in our experiment. We find that, when using our
human-centric algorithm, the rate of switching to larger boxes drops from 29.5% to 23.8% among
targeted packages, a relative improvement of 19.4% (p-value < 0.0001). Moreover, the reduction in
non-conforming behavior reduces the packing time of those targeted packages by 3.2 seconds, a rel-
ative improvement of 4.5% (p-value < 0.05). By combining our estimated improvements with actual
cost data from Alibaba, we show that our algorithm may result in annual savings of approximately
2.6 million dollars if applied to all warehouses of Alibaba.
    We also provide three strands of evidence that, while both types of deviations may exist in
switching-to-larger-boxes packages in the historical data, the majority of the gain from our algo-
rithm stems from reducing complexity deviation. First, we show that our algorithm reduces the
switching probability, and in turn the processing time, only for packages that have more than 1
item (i.e., packages that are more complex and in turn more likely to have complexity deviation).
Second, we also confirm that the estimated effect size does not change if we exclude packages where
workers are likely to have more information than the algorithm and wrap around certain fragile
items. Third, we find that our new algorithm reduces the probability of switching to boxes that are
at least two sizes larger from 13.0% to 10.2%, which accounts for around half of the improvement.
    Our main contributions can be summarized as follows. First, we provide suggestive evidence that
both information deviation and complexity deviation may exist in the same operational setting.
This reconciles two seemingly contradicting literature streams which show that human deviations

2.    Literature Review
Since our paper improves the bin packing algorithm by Alibaba, it is naturally related to the large
literature in Operations Research and Computer Science that studies the bin packing problem.
Bin packing is a classic NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem and has many variations for
different practical settings. Our research is mainly related to the three-dimensional bin packing
problem with multiple bin sizes. The problem is to pack a given set of three-dimensional items into
a given set of bins without overlapping. Various objective functions could be specified for the three-
dimensional bin packing algorithm, such as minimizing the wasted volume of bins, maximizing the
box fill rate, or minimizing the number of bins to use. Regardless of the selected objective, the
problem remains of formidable complexity. Although some papers propose exact algorithms for the
problem (Martello et al., 2000; Taylor et al., 2017), most work focuses on developing approximation
algorithms and corresponding heuristics (Hodgson, 1982). In most literature of the bin packing
problem, the items are modeled as perfect cuboid and are organized orthogonally in the bins.
  The classical literature for algorithm design, including but not limited to the bin packing litera-
ture, usually implicitly assumes that optimization algorithms finalize the managerial decisions and
that workers will implement the algorithmic prescriptions exactly. Our main contribution to the
literature of bin packing algorithm design is to provide empirical evidence that humans tend to
deviate from algorithmic prescriptions in a real-world setting and show that system-wise efficiency
can be improved by incorporating such predicted deviations in the algorithm design.
  Our work also contributes to a large literature on behavioral operations that studies worker
discretion from optimal solutions and behavioral biases in many operations settings, including
task sequence (Ibanez et al., 2018; Bendoly et al., 2014), demand forecasting (Kremer et al.,
2011), ordering (Schweitzer and Cachon, 2000; van Donselaar et al., 2010; Cui et al., 2015; Craig
et al., 2016; Moritz et al., 2019), inventory transshipment (Bostian et al., 2012), contract decisions
(Davis et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2019; Johnsen et al., 2019), picking (Batt and Gallino, 2019) and
providing services (Buell et al., 2016; Tan and Netessine, 2014). Such non-conforming behavior

typically results from workers’ behavioral biases or information mismatch between the worker and
the algorithm (see Bendoly et al. 2006 and Gino and Pisano 2008 for comprehensive reviews),
and the literature provides mixed results on whether such discretion may benefit or hurt the
overall productivity. For example, Ibanez et al. (2018) demonstrate that doctors tend to prioritize
tasks since they may want to group similar tasks together due to mental switching costs, but
such prioritization may reduce productivity in the long run. In contrast, van Donselaar et al.
(2010) find that store managers tend to deviate from advised ordering policies since they have
superior information, and conclude that such discretion may result in higher profits. Furthermore,
a recent stream literature, called algorithmic aversion, demonstrates that, even if humans know
that algorithms outperform humans and have the ability to follow algorithms, they might still be
reluctant to using algorithm prescriptions and prefer to believe in their own judgment if they see
the algorithms err (Dietvorst et al., 2015).
    Some of this behavioral operations literature has also proposed solutions by incorporating the
behavioral aspects into the algorithmic prescriptions. For instance, Boudreau et al. (2003) suggest
utilizing human responses to help explain the variations and anomalies and improve the operations
management system. van Donselaar et al. (2010) modify the automated order systems by learning
from the deviation behavior of retail store managers in ordering, and show that their method is
efficient to implement and may outperform store managers. Dietvorst et al. (2018) show that people
are more willing to use the algorithms if they are allowed to modify the algorithmic prescriptions.
Liu et al. (2018) use machine learning approaches to capture drivers’ routing behavior and predict
their actual delivery tour distance in the order assignment optimization model for last mile delivery.
Kim et al. (2015) include unobserved patient factors that physicians may consider in developing
the admission policy and show that the resulting policy improves the system performance com-
pared with a policy over only observed patient factors. There is also literature proposing different
interventions in workflow management to improve workers’ performance (Acimovic et al., 2018;
Song et al., 2017) or reduce discretionary behavior (Staats et al., 2017).
    We contribute to the behavioral operations literature by providing suggestive evidence in a new
setting (i.e., warehouse operations) that both information deviation and complexity deviation may
exist in one operational process. Moreover, different from the existing literature, we do not only
analyze how deviation happens and its impact, but also develop a low-cost method to update exist-
ing algorithms using machine learning techniques to reduce such deviations and improve overall
productivity. Last, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to implement such behav-
iorally adjusted algorithm in a randomized controlled experiment and demonstrate causally that
such algorithm adjustment based on human behavior can improve overall productivity. Given the
mixed evidence presented in the literature, we believe that it is valuable to provide more empirical

3.      Alibaba’s Warehouse Operations and Bin Packing Algorithms
3.1.     Overview of Alibaba’s Warehouse Management
As the largest e-commerce platform in China, Alibaba has a logistics affiliate named Cainiao
Network, which is one of the largest start-ups in China.3 Cainiao has built a collaborative platform
that connects logistics companies, merchants and consumers to improve the efficiency and quality
of logistics service both in China and globally. One of Cainiao’s major missions is to offer integrated
warehousing and delivery service to the merchants on Alibaba’s marketplaces. The merchants can
store their merchandise in Cainiao’s warehouses, and Cainiao helps manage the inventory, order
shipping and delivery process. Since Alibaba owns more than 50% of Cainiao Network, we simply
use Alibaba to refer to the company throughout the paper.
     Alibaba operates more than 200 warehouses with different service merchants or industries. These
warehouses use Alibaba’s warehouse management system, but the warehouse operations is often
outsourced to third-party warehousing service providers. Therefore, Alibaba’s warehouse manage-
ment system controls the operating process in the warehouses and provides algorithmic solutions
for many warehouse operations tasks, such as putaway locations, picking routes, packing box selec-
tions, etc. The workers from third-party companies receive such algorithmic prescriptions from
Alibaba’s system on their hand-held devices or monitors, and then perform the tasks according to
these prescriptions with, as we will show below, certain non-conformance.

3.2.     Alibaba’s Bin Packing Problem
For each order received by the warehouses, Alibaba’s warehouse management system will decide the
size and number of shipping boxes to pack the order by solving a bin packing problem. Alibaba’s
bin packing problem involves multiple objectives with different priorities. The primary objective
is to minimize the total number of boxes. If there are multiple solutions with the same number
of boxes, the secondary objective is to minimize the total cost of boxes. Since there are multiple
box sizes available in each warehouse, Alibaba’s bin packing problem can be formulated as a
three-dimensional multiple bin-size bin packing problem (3D-MBSBPP) with multiple objectives.

costs cj and has length Lj , width Wj and height Hj for j = 1, . . . , K. The maximum weight capacity
of box size Bj is Mj . Consider an order with N rectangular-shaped items, where each item i has
length li , width wi , height hi and mass mi for i = 1, . . . , N . We will decide xj , the number of boxes

3.3.    Alibaba’s Current Bin Packing Algorithm
In this section, we provide an overview of Alibaba’s current bin packing algorithm. Because the bin
packing problem is NP-hard, Alibaba developed a heuristic algorithm shown in Figure 1: For each
customer order received at the warehouse, the algorithm decides the number of boxes of each size to
use for packing the order and the items to pack in each box. There are two sets of input parameters
for this algorithm: the SKU parameters and the box parameters. The SKU parameters include the
length, width, height, weight and number of each SKU in an order. The box parameters include
the length, width, height, weight capacity and maximum box fill rate of each available box size.
The principle idea of Alibaba’s bin packing algorithm is to decompose the bin packing problem
into three sub-problems at different levels and develop an algorithm to solve each sub-problem.

  The most basic sub-problem is the positioning problem: Given a single box size and a packing
sequence of a set of items, determine the items to be fitted into the box and their positions and
orientations in the box, subject to the constraints of Alibaba’s bin packing problem. Alibaba
employs a greedy algorithm with two steps to solve this sub-problem. The first step is to split the
unoccupied box space into cuboids using the maximal rectangle algorithm. The second is to fit the
next item in the packing sequence into one of the cuboid free spaces according to some heuristic
rule. The weight capacity constraint and maximum fill rate constraint are also checked in this step.
The algorithm executes the two steps iteratively for each item until no more items in the packing
sequence can be fitted into the box. It finally returns the positions and orientations of the packed
items in the sequence, and the sequence of remaining items that have not been fitted into the box.
  The second sub-problem is the box-selection problem: Given a packing sequence of all the items
in the order, determine the number of boxes of each size to use. The algorithm for this problem
initiates an item sequence S with the sequence of all the items in the order. The algorithm first
checks whether all the items in S can be fitted into the largest box in the given order by calling the
positioning algorithm. If they can, the algorithm gradually lowers the box size and iteratively run
the positioning algorithm until it finds the minimum box size that can contain all the items in S.
Otherwise, S is updated to be the remaining sequence of items that have not been fitted into the
box, and the algorithm runs recursively with the updated S until there are no remaining items.
  The last sub-problem is the sequencing problem to optimize the packing sequence of items. The
objective of this problem is to minimize the total number of boxes, or to minimize the total box
cost if multiple solutions achieve the minimum number of boxes. The particle swarm optimization
(PSO) algorithm is currently used to solve this problem. During its search for the optimal packing
sequence, the box-selection algorithm and positioning algorithm as discussed above are called
iteratively to evaluate the performance of a packing sequence.

3.4.   Standard Packing Procedures and Practices in Alibaba’s Warehouses
We next describe the standard packing procedures for an order in Alibaba’s warehouses (depicted
in Figure 2), since they are closely related to how we design the bin packing algorithm, conduct the
experiment and analyze the results. When an order is received at the warehouse, the warehouse
management system obtains the information of all items in the order, and feeds it into the bin
packing algorithm. The algorithm then outputs the size and number of boxes needed for packing
the order and the box assignment of each item in the order. In practice, more than 90% of orders are
prescribed to be packed into one box, while the remaining orders are split into two or more boxes.
The system generates a packing slip, including an ID and the corresponding item list, for each
package. Then workers pick and sort the items into the picking containers. Each container contains
the items of a single packing slip. The containers are inventorized at the packing workstation.

     The standard packing procedures are as follows. A packing worker starts a packing process by
scanning the barcode on the packing slip. The item information and recommended box size will
show on the display. Then the worker picks the box for packing. Next, the worker scans the items
one by one and puts them inside the box. The worker may add some void fill materials for padding
in this process. If all the items have been scanned, confirmed undamaged and consistent with those
on the packing slip, the worker then wraps the box. Otherwise, the package will be sent to the
exception handling group. After wrapping the box, the worker scans the barcode of the box that
is actually used in packing so that the actually used box size is recorded. Finally, the mailing label
is printed, and the worker sticks the mailing label on the box and completes the packing process.
     There are a few points to highlight about the packing procedures. First, the order of the pro-
cedures are not interchangeable. The packing process of a package always starts with scanning
the barcode of the packing slip, followed by scanning the items, and ends with scanning the box
barcode. Second, the worker cannot use two boxes to pack a single package. This is not feasible
since only one mailing label can be printed for a single packing slip. In particular, if the worker
cannot fit all the items in the box, he/she must switch to a larger box but cannot simply use one
more box to pack the leftover items. Third, the workers can only see the recommended box size,
and they cannot observe any information about how to arrange the items in the recommended
box. Such restriction is due to the limited space in the packing station and the high cost of display
equipment. Fourth, Alibaba’s system only prescribes the box size and does not prescribe the use
of supplementary packing materials (e.g. item wrappers) to the workers. The rules for using sup-
plementary packing materials are usually given by warehouse managers. We observed that workers
typically follow those rules when deciding whether or not to use supplementary packing materials.

4.       Non-conforming Behavior in Bin Packing and Its Impact Prior to
         the Field Experiment
The bin packing algorithm has helped control the cost of packing materials and improve the
productivity of workers. However, we observe that workers occasionally do not use the box sizes
prescribed by the algorithm, but instead switch to larger or smaller ones than recommended. In this
section, we provide initial empirical evidence on how such non-conforming behavior is correlated
with productivity and box material cost. Note that the analysis for productivity in this section is
prior to our field experiment and in turn is only correlational. The causal impact of non-conforming
behavior on productivity is estimated in Section 7.2.

4.1.      Data
We conduct the analysis using the packing data of 1, 086, 441 packages from July 12, 2018 to July
31, 2018 prior to our experiment from four warehouses for the Tmall Supermarket of Alibaba
Group. Our unit of observation in our analysis is a package. The four warehouses were using the bin
packing algorithm as described in Section 3.3 before our experiment; and they are representative
of Alibaba’s warehouses in terms of size, facilities and operational processes.
     We exclude some observations in our analysis to ensure the stability and robustness of our
results. First, we exclude observations with packing time longer than 20 minutes (1.3% of all
packages). Exceptionally long packing time suggests that the worker suspended the packing process
for personal reasons, or the package was sent for exception handling5 before the packing process is
completed. Second, we exclude the packages with more than 25 items (0.8% of all packages). Such
packages typically include a large number of items of the same SKU. The workers may scan only 1
item and input manually the number of items of this SKU into the system, rather than scanning all
the items one by one as they do for packages with a small number of items. Therefore, the packing
procedures for the packages with an extremely large number of items may be different from others.
Our analysis of the field experiment in Section 7.1 also follows the same data exclusion criterion.

5.     Human-centric Bin Packing Algorithm
Given our findings that non-conforming behavior is common and is associated with longer packing
time and higher material cost, we develop a new algorithm to reduce non-conforming behavior and
increase productivity. In this section, we detail our human-centric bin packing algorithm, which is
built upon Alibaba’s current bin packing algorithm.

5.1.    Human Discretion Prediction Problem
The essential part of our algorithm is to predict when humans are likely to switch to a larger box.
We focus on switching to larger boxes rather than smaller boxes mainly for two reasons. First,
we get most of the benefits by reducing the cases of switching to larger boxes since switching-to-
larger-boxes deviations account for the majority (71.7%) of the non-conforming behavior. Second
and more importantly, our intervention with the algorithm must be simple enough to implement
in the field experiment. If we target deviations to larger boxes, where complexity deviation makes
up a significant proportion, our intervention can be as simple as lowering the maximum fill rate

box sizes and thus the rate of non-conforming behavior on average. The total cost in bin packing
C(R) consists of two types of cost: cost of conformance Cc (R) and cost of non-conformance Cn (R).
Cost of conformance Cc (R) mainly comes from the box material of recommended box sizes, since
recommending larger boxes generally improves workers’ conformance with prescriptions. Cost of
non-conformance Cn (R) includes the extra labor cost due to the longer packing time incurred by
switching behavior and extra box material cost due to possible use of much larger boxes than nec-
essary at a worker’s discretion. Figure 3(a) is a tailored Lundvall-Juran curve showing the trade-off
of the two types of cost in the bin packing setting. Increasing the maximum box fill rate R in the
algorithm results in smaller recommended box sizes and more non-conforming behavior. Therefore,
Cc (R) decreases while Cn (R) increases as we increase R. According to the theory of CoQ model,
there is an optimal maximum box fill rate R∗ that minimizes the total cost C(R).
  Moreover, we may improve on a single control R∗ if we leverage the heterogeneity in the packages
and set different R’s for different types of packages, as shown in Figure 3(b). We can divide the
packages into two groups according to whether workers switch to larger boxes or not when we set
R = 100% in the algorithm (i.e., Alibaba’s original threshold). For packages where workers will
switch to larger boxes when we set R = 100% in the algorithm, their total cost C 1 (R) consists of
their cost of conformance Cc1 (R) and cost of non-conformance Cn1 (R). For packages where workers
will not switch to larger boxes when we set R = 100% in the algorithm, their total cost C 2 (R)
consists of their cost of conformance Cc2 (R) and cost of non-conformance Cn2 (R). As shown in
Figure 3(b), we have distinct Lundvall-Juran curves for the two group of packages, and thus two

The analysis above provides a theoretical foundation for our prediction model and the key idea
of our new algorithm design as will be shown in Section 5.2. If our goal is to simply reduce non-
conforming behavior, we may recommend larger boxes for all packages (i.e., allowing all packages
to have a maximum box fill rate R < 100%) to reduce the switching-to-larger-boxes cases and reach
the goal. However, such an action would result in much larger box material costs because we may
recommend larger boxes even when workers would conform with the originally prescribed smaller
boxes. Therefore, since our goal is to reduce non-conforming behavior in order to reduce processing
time with the constraint of not significantly increasing material costs, we should only recommend
larger boxes when workers are likely to switch to larger boxes.
     Our prediction problem can be formulated as follows. For each package i in our training data,
we have a tuple of data (Xi , Yi ), where Xi is a vector of package features and box features for this
package and Yi is a binary output label representing workers’ switching behavior for this package.
Specifically, Yi = 1 if a worker packs the package i with a box larger than prescribed and Yi = 0
otherwise. The prediction problem is to predict Yi based on Xi by estimating the probability of
switching to a larger box Pi = P(Yi = 1|Xi ).

     5.1.1.   Feature Selection Overall, we use 43 features of the packages and boxes in our pre-
diction model. The features of a package-box pair to fit our prediction models can be classified into
three different categories. The first category contains package-specific features, the second category
contains box-specific features, and the third category contains joint features of the package and the
box. The package-specific features describe characteristics of the items in a given package i. There
are three types of package specific features: (1) summary features (mostly dimensional features) of
all the items in the package, including the total number of items, total volume of items and averages
of item volume and ratios of item dimensions; (2) box switching behavior associated with items in
the package, such as the probability of switching to larger boxes for packages that contained the
item in the last 7 days; and (3) features related to particular items in the package, such as the
largest SKU in the package. Table 7 in Appendix A summarizes all the package-specific features
with definitions. The box-specific features involve the box size switching history of the box size
recommended for package i, such as the probability of switching to larger boxes for packages where
the prescribed box size is the same as that for package i in the last 7 days. Table 8 in Appendix A
summarizes all the box-specific features with definitions. The joint feature of the package and the
box size is algo box fill rate, which is the fill rate of the prescribed box size for package i, defined
by the ratio of total volume of items in package i to the prescribed box volume.
     We exclude some potentially predictive features in our model for some practical reasons. First,
worker-specific features are not included in the prediction because they are not available when the
system runs the bin packing algorithm. In Alibaba’s warehouse operations practice, the bin packing
algorithm determines the box sizes for packing an order right after the order is received by the
warehouse. The packing tasks are randomly dispatched to the workers after the picking and sorting
processes. Therefore, we do not know which worker will pack a specific package when the system
runs the bin packing algorithm and decides the box sizes. Second, we also exclude features about
categories, special properties (e.g. liquid) or pre-packaging types of items in a package, since they
are not available or hard to compute in an online experiment environment. However, these features
can be partially captured by the box size switching history features of the items in a package.

experiment to train the prediction models and test their performance. The dataset is divided into

5.2.     The Human-centric Bin Packing Algorithm Design
Our human-centric bin packing algorithm modifies Alibaba’s current bin packing algorithm with
the prediction module above. This modification consists of two main parts. First, prior to running
the existing bin packing algorithm, we use our prediction model to identify packages where workers
are more than 50% likely to use boxes larger than recommended. Second, we target those packages
with deviation probability exceeding 50% and adjust their algorithmic prescriptions by lowering
their maximum box fill rate R. We choose the threshold cutoff at 50% for two main reasons. First,
it is a natural bar for binary classification given that the prediction score could be viewed as the

  Moreover, we adjust the algorithmic prescriptions for those targeted packages by re-running
Alibaba’s current bin packing algorithm with a constraint that the box fill rate should not exceed
70% (note that the original algorithm constrains R = 100%). In this case, the new algorithm will
enlarge the recommended box sizes for those targeted packages where the original prescribed fill
rates are larger than 70%. For targeted packages where the original prescribed fill rates are smaller
than 70%, our new algorithm will not change the prescriptions. We set the maximum box fill rate
R to be 70% to balance the benefit of recommending larger boxes for proper cases (i.e. saving
processing time) and the cost of doing this for unnecessary cases (i.e. increasing box material cost).
If we choose a higher ratio, we may leave money on the table since our new algorithm may leave out
some packages where workers switch to larger boxes. If we choose a lower ratio, we may recommend
larger boxes for some packages where workers may not deviate originally. After discussing with
the engineering team, we choose 70% empirically to balance the two sides. Note that we do not
know whether the choices of 50% and 70% as cutoffs are optimal given that we cannot simulate
the entire system before the experiment. Therefore, we interpret the results of this algorithm, if it
improves operations, as a lower bound on such improvement.
  The detailed description of the algorithm is given in Algorithm 1. For each arriving package i,
we first run Alibaba’s bin packing algorithm with the maximum box fill rate R = 100% to get a
recommended box size k. Then we run the prediction algorithm for package i and box size k. The
algorithm outputs a prediction score indicating the probability that a larger box than size k will be

     Given our algorithm design, we argue that our human-centric algorithm may theoretically
improve the operational performance in two different ways, depending on the types of deviations.
For complexity deviation, our algorithm may reduce the prescribed box fill rate and thus the com-
plexity of the packing task by setting a lower maximum box fill rate for the targeted packages in
the algorithm. This may help reduce the probability of switching to larger boxes and improve the
productivity of workers. For information deviation, our algorithm may identify the packages that
contain fragile items that need wrappers given their box size switching history, and recommend
proper box sizes with wrapping materials taken into account. In this sense, our algorithm may
incorporate human wisdom and improve the algorithmic prescriptions in a low-cost manner that
a conventional bin packing algorithm cannot do. It turns out that the majority of the benefits of
our algorithm come from reducing complexity deviation, as we will discuss in Section 7.1.

7.      Main Results
We present the results of the field experiment in this section. We first analyze the effects of our
human-centric algorithm on non-conforming behavior, productivity and box material cost for tar-
geted packages. Then we re-estimate the impact of non-conforming behavior on productivity but
now causally by leveraging the field experiment. Finally, we analyze the economic impact of our
human-centric algorithm at Alibaba.

the efficacy of adjusting algorithmic prescriptions based on predicted deviation behavior of workers.

7.2.     Causal Impact of Non-conforming Behavior on Productivity
In this section, we estimate the impact of non-conforming behavior on productivity causally by
leveraging the field experiment. Notice that the assignment of an order (and thus a package) to
treatment and control groups is random, and the group assignment affects the packing time only by
affecting the non-conforming behavior. Therefore, we can use group assignment as an instrumental
variable to the binary variable of non-conformance in Specification (1) and estimate the causal
impact of non-conforming behavior on productivity.
     Table 6 shows the OLS results of Specification (1) using data of the targeted packages in the
experiment.17 Column (1) shows the result without any instrumental variable as in Column (1)
of Table 2. It is estimated that non-conforming behavior will increase the packing time by 7.5
seconds, and the effect is statistically significant. In Column (2), we use the group assignment as
the instrumental variable to Non-conformanceikt in Specification (1), which yields a causal estimate
of the effect of non-conformance. Column (2) shows that non-conforming behavior increases the
packing time by 49.2 seconds, and the effect is statistically significant. Therefore, we confirm that
non-conforming behavior indeed causes an increase in packing time and thus loss in productivity.

bin packing. Using the observational packing data from four warehouses of Alibaba Group in July
2018, we quantify the existence of two types of non-conforming behavior: we observe that workers

use a box size different from prescribed for 5.8% of the packages, with 1.6% of the packages where
workers switch to smaller boxes (information deviation) and 4.2% of the packages where workers
switch to larger boxes (both information deviation and complexity deviation). We show that non-
conforming behavior increases the box material cost and lowers the productivity of workers.
     We then propose a method to revise the algorithm design by building a machine learning model
to predict humans’ non-conforming behavior and using the predictions to adjust the algorithmic
prescriptions. We conduct a randomized experiment in four warehouses of Alibaba Group for two
weeks to causally evaluate the performance of our algorithm design. In particular, for the targeted
packages where our revised algorithm might adjust the original prescriptions, the non-conformance
rate is lowered by 5.7 percentage points (a 19.4% relative improvement) and the packing time is
reduced by 4.5% relatively. We show that the majority of our new algorithm’s benefits arises from
reducing complexity deviations.
     Our results highlight the importance of integrating behavioral components with the optimization
algorithm design to improve its realized performance in practice. In particular, we show the effec-
tiveness of utilizing implementation data of workers as feedback to the optimization algorithm to
adjust the prescriptions and reduce non-conformance using machine learning techniques. This idea
can also be applicable to algorithm designs in other operational processes. Moreover, our study
also demonstrates that the non-conforming behavior can be beneficial in some cases but harmful
in others. Companies should not simply avoid all deviations through regulation, but should figure
out the reasons for non-conforming behavior before deciding how to make an improvement. If the
deviations are information deviations that result from lack of information in the algorithm design,
the companies should learn from these deviations to acquire new information and use it to improve
the algorithms rather than force workers to follow the algorithmic prescriptions. However, if the
deviations are complexity deviations that arise from humans’ aversion, inability or discretion and
lead to lower efficiency and increased cost, the companies should work on making the algorithms
more accessible to workers to reduce such non-conforming behavior.
     Our work also has certain limitations that could be addressed by future research. First, we
classify deviations into information deviations and complexity deviations, since this classification is
sufficient for the analysis in the paper. In practice, different data and different motives of deviations
may be present in other settings, which requires researchers to refine the current classification so
that it can be applied to the new settings. Second, another potentially interesting intervention
that we did not explore is displaying to workers the recommended guidance on arranging items
in the box. It is interesting to study how such types of interventions can improve operations in
different settings. Third, we were not able to account for worker heterogeneity in our algorithm
design since Alibaba’s system runs the bin packing algorithm before randomly assigning the packing

jobs to the workers. As our heterogeneous treatment effect analysis in Appendix E suggests, the
differences among workers in their warehouses or working shifts may result in heterogeneity in the
performance of our human-centric algorithm. Therefore, accounting for worker heterogeneity in
the algorithm design may further improve the algorithm’s performance and is worth investigating
when possible. Fourth, we do not completely distinguish information deviation from complexity
deviation in our setting due to data limitation. It would be valuable to distinguish them at a
more granular level, or explore other more distinguishable classifications in the future. Finally, we
mainly focus on the bin packing process in this study. Our heterogeneous treatment effect analysis
suggests that the benefits of accounting for human’s behavior in the algorithm design may vary with
different characteristics of an operations process. Therefore, it would be interesting to investigate
how human-centric algorithm design may help improve operations in other settings.

it is on weekends or not.
     • Whether a package is packed during busy hours in a day. The warehouses operate around 20 hours per
